The 50/30/20 budget, in plain numbers

17 October 2026 · 2 min read · Jump to the calculator

Most budgets fail because they are too detailed. Twenty categories, a spreadsheet, and by the third week nobody is updating it. The 50/30/20 split works because it has only three buckets.

The three buckets

  • Needs, about 50%. The things you must pay: rent or bond, utilities, groceries, transport, insurance and minimum debt payments.
  • Wants, about 30%. Everything that makes life better but is optional: eating out, streaming, clothes beyond the basics, holidays.
  • Saving and debt, about 20%. Your emergency fund, retirement, investments, and any payments above the minimum on debt.

The percentages are of your take-home pay, the amount that actually lands in your account.

A worked example

Say you take home $4,000 a month.

BucketGuideExample spendShare of pay
Needs$2,000$2,20055%
Wants$1,200$1,10028%
Saving and debt$800$70018%

That is a fairly typical picture: needs run a little high, wants are under control, and saving lands just short of 20%. The $700 left each month is $8,400 a year. Saved every month for ten years at an assumed 5% a year, it grows to about $108,700.

When the split doesn’t fit

For many people, especially in expensive cities or early in their career, needs take well over half of their pay. That doesn’t mean you’ve failed. Use the split as a direction, not a test:

  1. Start by saving something, even 5%, automatically on payday.
  2. Each time your pay rises, send half the increase to saving before you get used to spending it.
  3. Look at your two biggest needs once a year. Insurance, phone contracts and loans can often be renegotiated.

Assumptions behind these results

  • The 50/30/20 split is a guide, not a rule: about 50% of take-home pay for needs, 30% for wants and 20% for saving and paying off debt.
  • Whatever is left after needs and wants is treated as money you could save or use to clear debt.
  • The 10-year figure assumes you save that amount at the end of every month at a fixed 5% a year, compounded monthly. Real returns vary.
  • Tax, irregular costs (car repairs, gifts) and pay rises are not included.
